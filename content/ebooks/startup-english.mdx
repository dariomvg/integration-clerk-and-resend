# Business English for Startups

## Introduction

Working in an international startup requires more than technical skills.

You may need to explain an idea to an investor, present a product to a customer, negotiate with a partner, or collaborate with teammates across different countries.

You don't need complicated vocabulary.

You need clear, natural, and confident communication.

---

# Chapter 1: Introducing Yourself

A professional introduction should be short.

A useful structure is:

**Who you are → What you do → What you're working on**

For example:

> I'm a software developer focused on building web applications. I'm currently working on a SaaS product that helps small teams manage their projects.

Avoid giving your entire background.

Give people a reason to continue the conversation.

---

# Chapter 2: Talking About Your Company

When describing a startup, focus on the problem and the customer.

A simple structure:

**We help [customer] do [result] by [solution].**

For example:

> We help small businesses automate their invoicing by connecting their existing accounting tools.

This is usually more effective than listing technical features.

---

# Chapter 3: Meetings

Meetings become easier when you have phrases ready for common situations.

### Starting a meeting

> Let's get started.

> Thanks everyone for joining.

> Let's quickly go over today's agenda.

### Sharing an opinion

> I think we should...

> From my perspective...

> I believe the main issue is...

### Agreeing

> That makes sense.

> I agree with that approach.

> I think that's a good direction.

### Disagreeing

> I see it differently.

> I'm not sure that's the best approach.

> I understand your point, but I think we should consider...

Professional disagreement should focus on the idea, not the person.

---

# Chapter 4: Asking Questions

Good questions make conversations more productive.

Useful expressions include:

> Could you clarify that?

> What do you mean by...?

> Could you give me an example?

> What would that look like in practice?

> What is the main priority here?

Don't be afraid to ask questions.

Clarifying something early is usually better than misunderstanding it later.

---

# Chapter 5: Giving Updates

When reporting progress, keep the structure simple.

**Completed → Current → Blocked**

For example:

> I've finished the authentication flow. I'm currently working on the billing integration. I'm blocked by an issue with the payment API.

This structure works well for standups and async communication.

---

# Chapter 6: Presentations

A strong presentation usually has three parts:

### 1. Problem

Explain what is currently difficult.

### 2. Solution

Explain how your product or idea addresses it.

### 3. Result

Explain what changes for the customer.

Avoid filling every slide with information.

One clear idea is usually better than several competing ideas.

---

# Chapter 7: Negotiations

Negotiation isn't about winning every point.

It's about finding an agreement that works for both sides.

Useful phrases include:

> What would work best for you?

> Is there any flexibility on this?

> Could we explore another option?

> What would you need from us to move forward?

> Let me think about that and get back to you.

Don't feel pressured to answer immediately.

---

# Chapter 8: Talking About Pricing

When discussing price, be direct.

Instead of:

> I'm sorry, but our price is...

Say:

> The plan is $49 per month.

If someone asks why, explain the value.

For example:

> The price includes unlimited projects, priority support, and all future updates.

Confidence comes from knowing what you're offering and why it matters.

---

# Chapter 9: Writing Professional Emails

Business emails should usually be concise.

A useful structure is:

**Greeting → Context → Request → Next step**

For example:

> Hi Alex,  
>
> I'm following up on our conversation about the integration. We've completed the technical review and are ready to move forward.  
>
> Could you send over the API documentation when you have a chance?  
>
> Thanks,  
> Daniel

Short messages are often easier to read and respond to.

---

# Chapter 10: Networking

Networking isn't simply collecting contacts.

It's about building genuine professional relationships.

Useful conversation starters include:

> What are you currently working on?

> How did you get into this field?

> What kind of projects are you interested in?

> What are you learning right now?

Focus on being curious.

You don't need to immediately ask for something.

---

# Chapter 11: Handling Mistakes

Mistakes happen in every professional environment.

The important part is communicating them clearly.

A useful structure is:

**Acknowledge → Explain → Action**

For example:

> I noticed an issue with yesterday's deployment. I've identified the cause and I'm working on a fix. I'll update the team once it's resolved.

Avoid unnecessary excuses.

Take responsibility and focus on the solution.

---

# Chapter 12: Sounding More Natural

You don't need to use complicated expressions.

Natural business English often uses simple language.

Instead of:

> We will initiate the implementation process shortly.

Say:

> We'll start implementing it soon.

Instead of:

> I am in possession of the required information.

Say:

> I have the information we need.

Clear English is professional English.

---

# Useful Expressions

Keep these phrases available during conversations:

| Situation | Expression |
|---|---|
| Clarifying | "Could you clarify that?" |
| Agreeing | "That makes sense." |
| Disagreeing | "I see it differently." |
| Delaying | "Let me get back to you." |
| Confirming | "Just to make sure..." |
| Suggesting | "What if we..." |
| Summarizing | "So, to summarize..." |
| Closing | "Let's move forward with that." |

# A Simple Practice Routine

Spend 20 minutes a day practicing.

### 5 minutes

Read a business article or startup newsletter.

### 5 minutes

Write a short summary.

### 5 minutes

Explain the idea out loud.

### 5 minutes

Review new expressions.

Consistency will improve your confidence more than memorizing hundreds of isolated words.

---

# Final Thoughts

Business English isn't about sounding sophisticated.

It's about making your ideas easy to understand.

Speak clearly.

Ask questions.

Keep your messages concise.

Listen carefully.

And don't wait until your English is perfect before using it professionally.

Confidence comes from communication practice, not from knowing every word.
